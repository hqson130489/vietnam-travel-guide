Getting Around Vietnam: Planes, Trains, Buses and Ride-Hailing

Updated September 2026 · 3407 words

Vietnam is long and thin, and the distance between the places you want to see is the single thing first-time visitors most often underestimate. This guide answers the questions we hear most about moving between Hanoi, Ninh Binh, Hue, Da Nang, Hoi An, Ho Chi Minh City, Ha Long Bay, Sapa, the Mekong Delta and Phu Quoc: when a flight is genuinely worth it, when the train is the point of the journey, when a sleeper bus or a private car makes more sense, and how to get around once you arrive. The short answer

Decide one leg at a time rather than picking a single favourite mode for the whole trip. Ask three questions about each leg: how far is it, is the scenery part of the experience, and what does the arrival time cost you in sleep or in a lost day?

  • Long hops: fly. Hanoi to Ho Chi Minh City is roughly a two-hour flight against a very long ride, and Da Nang to Ho Chi Minh City is a similar gap. Flying buys you days, not hours.
  • Medium hops with good rail: take the train, especially Hue to Da Nang, where the coastal stretch over the Hai Van Pass is one of the best things you will see in Vietnam.
  • Short, awkward legs: a private car or a limousine van. Hanoi to Ninh Binh, Hue to Hoi An and Hanoi to Ha Long Bay all fall into this group, where the door-to-door time of a direct vehicle beats the faff of a station or an airport.
  • Overnight legs: a soft sleeper train or a sleeper bus can replace a hotel night. A train gives you a bed and a corridor; a bus gives you a reclined berth and less room.
  • Inside cities: ride-hailing apps for almost everything, plus your legs for the old-town cores and the waterfronts.

Domestic flights

Vietnam's domestic network is busy and the main trunk routes are genuinely useful. Hanoi, Da Nang and Ho Chi Minh City are linked by several flights a day, which means you can usually pick a departure that suits your day rather than planning the whole trip around one flight. Other routes worth knowing about are Hanoi or Ho Chi Minh City to Phu Quoc, Nha Trang, Da Lat and Hue, plus the long haul between the two big cities.

Fly when the alternative costs you a day or a night you would rather spend somewhere else. Do not fly a short leg just because the fare looks low: once you add the drive to the airport, the check-in buffer, the baggage fee and the transfer at the other end, a two-hour drive is often faster door to door.

Baggage rules to check before you pay

Check what your fare actually includes. On several domestic fares the base price covers a small cabin bag only, with a checked allowance sold separately and priced by weight. Budget carriers also enforce cabin size and weight more strictly than you may be used to, and the gate fee for an oversized bag is much higher than the online price. If you are carrying dive gear, a surfboard or a bicycle, arrange it in advance rather than at the counter.

How early to arrive

Domestic terminals are smaller and quicker than international ones, but they are also crowded at peak times. Allow around 90 minutes before departure for a domestic flight, and more on public holidays or when you are travelling with checked bags. Add the drive to the airport on top: in Hanoi and Ho Chi Minh City that drive can take far longer than you expect.

Schedule changes are the real risk

Airlines retime and occasionally cancel domestic flights, sometimes only a day or two ahead. Keep a buffer between a domestic flight and anything that cannot move, such as an international departure, a cruise boarding or a fixed-date tour start. Book through a channel that will actually contact you when a flight moves, and check your booking again a week before you fly rather than trusting the confirmation email from months earlier.

Trains

The Reunification Express is the line that runs the length of the country, and it is less a single famous train than a timetable of services sharing one track. Trains are slower than buses on paper and often slower than you expect in practice, but they are roomier, you can walk around, and the coastal sections are worth the time.

The classes, honestly

  • Hard seat: the cheapest option and a genuine local experience on short daylight hops. Fine for a couple of hours, tiring for a night.
  • Soft seat: an air-conditioned carriage with reclining seats. A good choice for daytime legs of three to six hours.
  • Hard sleeper: six berths in an open bay, three on each side. Cheap, sociable, and noisier; the top berth is the cheapest and the least convenient.
  • Soft sleeper: four berths in a closed cabin with a door. This is what most visitors want for overnight legs, and it is the class that sells out first.

Some routes also run privately operated tourist carriages attached to the scheduled service. They cost more and book differently, but the cabins are usually a little more comfortable.

Which legs are scenic

Hue to Da Nang over the Hai Van Pass is the classic: the train runs along the coast with the mountains on one side and the sea on the other, and it is short enough to do in daylight without losing a day. Sit on the sea side if you can. Other stretches with real payoff are the coastal running south of Da Nang towards Nha Trang, and the northern approach into Ninh Binh and further up towards the mountains.

Booking ahead

On popular dates, soft sleeper cabins on the scenic and overnight routes can sell out days or weeks ahead, and Tet and the summer school holiday are the tightest periods. Book as soon as your dates are fixed, and earlier for a family or a group who need cabins together. Tickets are easiest to buy through the official railway channels or through your hotel or a local agent, who can also explain which station you actually need and how to read the carriage and berth numbers on your ticket.

Buses and vans

Vietnam's road network is the workhorse of local travel, and two vehicle types matter most to visitors: sleeper buses and limousine vans.

Sleeper buses have three rows of reclined berths, sometimes in individual pods. They cover long routes overnight and are often the cheapest way to move a long distance. You usually get a thin pillow and a blanket, a bottle of water, and a stop or two for food and toilets. Space is tight, luggage goes underneath, and tall travellers should expect to fold.

Limousine vans are nine to sixteen seat vans with wide reclining seats, sold as a premium step above the local bus. They run frequently on short routes such as Hanoi to Ninh Binh, Hanoi to Ha Long Bay and Hai Phong, and Hanoi to Sapa, and they often pick up and drop off at hotels rather than at a bus station. For those legs they are frequently the best value option on the whole trip.

When a bus or van beats the train

Take the road when the rail timetable does not fit your day, when the train station is far from where you are staying, or when the leg is short enough that a van is simply quicker. Hue to Hoi An is the clearest example: a few hours by road against a train that drops you at Da Nang and leaves you to finish the journey. Ninh Binh to Hue is another route where the overnight bus is a practical alternative when the trains are full.

Comfort, motion sickness and safety

Mountain roads and the winding passes are the usual culprits, and a berth near the middle of the bus moves less than one over the wheels. If you are prone to motion sickness, take medication before you board rather than after, sit forward where you can, and avoid heavy meals. On safety, book with operators your hotel or a reputable agent recommends rather than whoever shouts loudest at the station, keep your valuables on you rather than in the hold, and use the seat belt where one is fitted. Driving overnight is common and not risk-free.

The night bus trade-off

A night bus saves a hotel night and hands you a full day at your destination. It also costs you sleep. Arriving at six in the morning after a broken night on a reclined berth is not the same as arriving rested, so do not schedule a long trek, a cooking class or a boat departure for the morning after an overnight road leg. If you are only in the country for a short trip, one overnight bus is usually enough.

Getting around cities

Ride-hailing apps

Grab and similar apps have transformed city travel for visitors. You set the destination in the app, you see the price before you accept it, and you do not have to negotiate. Two vehicle types matter: the car, which is comfortable and air-conditioned and the right choice with luggage or in the rain, and the motorbike, which is faster in heavy traffic and cheaper. A motorbike ride means sitting on the back with a helmet the driver provides; it is a normal way to get around and not for everyone.

Pay however the app allows. Cards are usually accepted in the app, but many drivers prefer cash, and a small note beats a large one. Keep some small change for the trips where the signal drops or the driver asks for cash.

Taxis, touts and cyclos

Use a metered taxi arranged by your hotel or through a reputable operator, and make sure the meter is running. The main thing to avoid is the driver who approaches you inside a terminal or outside a station and offers a fixed price: that price is usually several times the metered fare, and the same goes for anyone who tells you the app is not working or that your hotel has closed. Walk a little away from the doors, open the app, and you remove the whole conversation.

In Hue, a cyclo ride around the Citadel and along the Perfume River is a slow, pleasant way to see the old city, and it is a fair way for a local operator to earn. Agree the price and the duration before you sit down. In Hoi An, the old town is compact and much of the centre is closed to traffic in the evening, so walking is the best way to see it: the streets between the Japanese Bridge, the assembly halls and the riverfront are only a few minutes apart, and there is no reason to take a vehicle at all.

Renting a motorbike

Renting a scooter is one of the great pleasures of travelling in Vietnam, and it is also the fastest way to turn a good trip into a bad one. Rules, licence expectations and enforcement vary by area and change over time, so check the current requirements before you rent rather than relying on what another traveller told you last year. An international driving permit is commonly expected alongside your home licence, but treat that as something to verify, not as a certainty.

Wear a proper helmet, not the thin decorative kind, and check that the rental includes one that fits. Inspect the bike and photograph existing damage before you ride away, and be clear about what happens if you damage it. Insurance is where the real gap sits: rental bikes are often covered only for third-party liability, which means damage to the bike or to you may not be covered at all, and many travel insurance policies exclude motorbike injuries, particularly if you were riding without a licence that the insurer recognises. Read your own policy and ask your rental shop directly, in writing if you can, before you get on the bike.

Boats and ferries

Boats are rarely just transport in Vietnam; they are usually the activity itself.

Ha Long Bay and Lan Ha Bay. Overnight cruises are the standard way to see the limestone scenery, and one night is the minimum, with two nights giving a much calmer experience. Cabins range from simple to genuinely luxurious, and the price usually bundles meals, a guide, kayaking and cave visits, so the headline number is not directly comparable with a hotel room. Check what is included before you compare boats, and check whether the itinerary goes to Lan Ha Bay, which is quieter than the main Ha Long routes.

The Mekong Delta. Day tours from Can Tho, Cai Be or My Tho use small boats to reach the floating markets, canals and fruit gardens. The floating markets are a morning affair, so an early start is worth it, and a smaller boat gets you closer to the action than a large one.

Phu Quoc ferries. Fast ferries connect Phu Quoc with the mainland at Ha Tien and Rach Gia. They are quick and reasonably comfortable, and they can be rough in the wrong weather.

Weather is the thing to plan around. Rough seas and storms cause ferry and cruise cancellations, most likely in the rainy months, and no operator will sail if the conditions are unsafe. Never book a ferry for the same day as an international flight home, and keep a night on the mainland in hand before a crossing if your schedule is tight.

Apps, data and realistic transfer times

Sort out data before you land. A tourist SIM or eSIM with a month of data typically costs $6–20 (estimate, September 2026), and an eSIM means you can have it working before you leave the airport. With data you can use ride-hailing, maps, translation and restaurant reviews without thinking about it, and you can pay for most small things in cash without hunting for an ATM every day.

Cash still matters. Markets, small eateries, cyclo drivers and many family-run shops do not take cards, and having a few small notes makes daily life much easier. Use cards for hotels, larger restaurants and bigger purchases, and keep a modest amount of cash for everything else.

How long transfers really take

The single most common planning mistake is treating the map distance as the journey time. Hanoi and Ho Chi Minh City both have dense traffic, and a ten-kilometre trip across either city can easily take an hour at the wrong time of day. Add a buffer to every transfer, and a generous one on the day of a flight.

  • Airport to city centre in Hanoi or Ho Chi Minh City: allow an hour, more at peak times. A ride-hailing transfer is typically $12–25, and a pre-booked private car $20–35 (estimates, September 2026).
  • Station, bus stop and pier transfers: allow extra for finding the right entrance, which is often not the one on the map.
  • A domestic flight day is effectively half a day gone, even on a short route.
  • A long overnight leg is a whole night gone, and the morning after is not a full-strength morning.

Build the trip around fewer, longer stays rather than trying to fit everything in. Three nights in one place beats three one-night stops, and the transport you skip is time you spend actually seeing the country.

Frequently asked questions

Is it better to fly or take the train from Hanoi to Da Nang?

Fly if time is short, and take the train if you want the journey to count. The flight is quick but adds airport time at both ends and a transfer into the city. The train is far slower, though a soft sleeper overnight turns the travelling into a night's accommodation and delivers you to Da Nang in the morning.

How far in advance should I book Reunification Express tickets?

Book as soon as your dates are fixed. Soft sleeper cabins on the popular overnight routes and the scenic Hue to Da Nang stretch are the first to sell out, and holiday periods are tightest of all. Booking days ahead is usually enough outside peak season, but weeks ahead is safer for families or groups who need cabins together.

Are sleeper buses comfortable enough for a first trip to Vietnam?

They are workable rather than luxurious. You get a reclined berth, a pillow and a blanket, but space is tight and tall travellers will feel folded. Most people manage one or two overnight buses without trouble. If you sleep badly on the move, pay more for a soft sleeper train on the same route.

Is Grab cheaper than a taxi in Vietnam?

Usually, and the bigger advantage is certainty: you see the price before you accept, so there is nothing to argue about at the end of the ride. A short city car trip typically runs $1.50–4 and a motorbike trip $0.80–2.50 (estimates, September 2026). Metered taxis booked through your hotel are a good fallback.

Do I need an international driving permit to rent a motorbike?

Requirements vary by area and change over time, and enforcement is not uniform, so check the current rules before you rent and do not rely on another traveller's experience. What matters more is your insurance: many travel policies exclude motorbike injuries, sometimes entirely when the rider's licence is not one the insurer recognises. Read your own policy first.

How much buffer time should I allow between transfers?

More than you think. Allow around 90 minutes before a domestic flight, an hour for an airport run in Hanoi or Ho Chi Minh City, and extra for any transfer to a station or pier. If a ferry, cruise or international flight follows, keep a night in hand rather than chaining them on the same day. Weather and traffic both cancel plans regularly.
