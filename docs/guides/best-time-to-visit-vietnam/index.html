The Best Time to Visit Vietnam, Region by Region

Updated September 2026 · 3483 words

Vietnam is not one climate. It is a country stretching more than 1,600 kilometres from the mountains on the Chinese border to the flat, tropical south, and the weather in Hanoi in January has almost nothing in common with the weather in Phu Quoc in the same week. That is why "the best time to visit Vietnam" is really a question about your route. This guide splits the country into its three climate zones, shows what each month usually feels like, and explains what that means for a 7–10 day trip.

This climate summary is a planning guide based on typical patterns rather than a forecast. It was reviewed in September 2026. Weather varies year to year, so check a reliable weather forecast before you book flights, boats or mountain treks.

Why Vietnam has three climates, not one

Two monsoon systems do most of the work. From roughly October to March, a northeast monsoon pushes cool, damp air down from southern China across the north of the country. During those same months the south is in its dry, sunny season. From roughly May to October, a southwest monsoon arrives off the Indian Ocean, soaking the south and the central highlands while the north gets its heaviest rain and typhoon season builds along the coast.

Geography finishes the job. The Truong Son range separates the narrow central coast from the interior, so Hue and Da Nang can be under grey rain while the Mekong Delta is six hours away and bone dry. The mountains around Sapa and Ha Giang sit high enough to be genuinely cold in winter, which surprises a lot of first-time visitors who pack only for beaches.

The practical consequence is simple: there is no single best month for the whole country. In February you can be wearing a rain shell in a damp, grey Hanoi while Phu Quoc is hot and dry. In July the reverse is true. Pick the region that matches your travel dates, or pick the dates that match the region you most want to see.

Northern Vietnam: Hanoi, Ninh Binh, Ha Long Bay, Sapa and Ha Giang

The north has four recognisable seasons, including a real winter. Spring and autumn are the most comfortable windows for sightseeing.

Spring: March and April

Temperatures climb steadily and the landscape turns green. March often keeps the last of the winter mist and light drizzle, especially around Ninh Binh and the Red River delta, while April usually brings clearer skies and mild, pleasant days. This is a strong window for Hanoi, Ninh Binh and a first trip to Ha Long Bay, and it suits the northern mountains before the summer rain arrives.

Autumn: October and November

For most travellers this is the best time in the north. Humidity drops, the skies clear, daytime temperatures are comfortable and evenings are cool enough for a light layer. September and early October are also when the rice terraces around Sapa and Mu Cang Chai are at their most photogenic, though early autumn can still bring heavy rain. November is usually crisp and dry, and it is an excellent month for Ha Giang.

Winter: December to February

Hanoi turns cool and often grey, with drizzly spells that can hang on for days. It is rarely freezing in the city, but it is damp enough that a warm jacket matters. Further north and higher up it is a different story: Sapa and Ha Giang can be genuinely cold, with fog, strong wind and occasional frost at altitude. Mountain views that look spectacular in the photos can be hidden behind cloud for a whole day. Trekking is still possible, but pack for cold and keep plans flexible.

Summer: May to August

Hot, humid and wet. Days are sticky, afternoons often end in heavy showers, and the heat builds through June and July. The bigger issue is the coast: from July into September the north is in typhoon season, and Ha Long Bay overnight cruises can be cancelled at short notice when port authorities close the bay to boats. Landslides and road closures are possible in Ha Giang and other mountain provinces after prolonged rain.

Planning around Ha Long Bay. If a boat trip is the centrepiece of your northern leg, avoid late July to September where you can, and if you cannot, build in a spare day and keep your onward flight flexible. Cruise operators usually refund or reschedule when the bay is closed, but they cannot create good weather.

Central Vietnam: Hue, Da Nang and Hoi An

The centre runs on a different clock from both the north and the south. Its wet season arrives late in the year, not in the middle of it.

February to April: usually the best window

This is the driest and most pleasant stretch of the year for Hue, Da Nang and Hoi An. Days are warm, evenings are cooler, and the old towns and imperial sites are comfortable to walk. Rain is possible but usually short. April starts to feel like beach weather, and the Hai Van Pass between Hue and Da Nang is often clear.

May to August: hot, dry and good for the beach

Daytime temperatures push into the low-to-mid thirties and the sea is warm and calm. Da Nang and the beaches near Hoi An are at their best. The trade-off is heat: sightseeing in the middle of the day is uncomfortable, so plan early mornings and late afternoons, and treat the middle hours as pool or beach time. Storms become more likely towards the end of August.

September to November: the wet season

Rainfall climbs sharply, and the wettest months are usually October and November, when tropical storms and typhoons can bring serious flooding to low-lying areas. Hoi An sits beside a river and parts of the old town have flooded in past years; Hue can see standing water in the streets. Flights and trains are sometimes delayed, and boat trips to the Cham Islands are often cancelled. Early September is usually the gentler end of this window.

December and January: cooler and sometimes damp

The rain eases but the air stays moist, and Da Nang and Hue can have grey, drizzly days. It is not beach weather, and the sea is often rough, but the region is quieter and cheaper and the temperatures are pleasant for walking. Bring a light jacket for the evening.

Worth remembering: the centre's wettest months are the south's rainy-season tail and the north's best autumn. If you are travelling in October or November, the north and the south are usually the safer bets, with the centre kept short and flexible.

Southern Vietnam: Ho Chi Minh City, Mekong Delta, Phu Quoc and Mui Ne

The south is hot all year, with two seasons rather than four: dry and rainy. Temperatures rarely drop below the low twenties, and humidity is a constant.

Dry season: roughly December to April

Blue skies, low rainfall and the most reliable beach weather of the year on Phu Quoc and along the coast at Mui Ne. December and January are the most comfortable months in Ho Chi Minh City; by March and April the heat peaks and afternoons can be fierce. This is the busiest stretch for island resorts, especially around Christmas, New Year and Tet.

Rainy season: roughly May to November

Rain usually arrives as a short, heavy afternoon or early-evening downpour rather than all-day drizzle. Mornings are frequently sunny, and an hour later the streets are dry again. It is entirely possible to enjoy a full day of sightseeing with one soaking in the middle. The wettest months are usually September and October, when the Mekong Delta can flood and rough seas occasionally disrupt ferries to the islands.

What that means in practice

You do not need to avoid the southern rainy season, but you should plan around it. Keep a light rain jacket with you, book flexible day trips rather than fixed long-distance transfers on your last day, and leave the Mekong Delta and island hopping for the drier months if those are the priority. Mui Ne's steady winds arrive with the drier season, which is why the kitesurfing crowds come then.

What this means for a 7–10 day trip

In seven to ten days you can comfortably cover one region in depth or two regions with one internal flight. Trying to see the north, the centre and the south in that time means spending a large share of your holiday in airports, and it leaves no room for the weather to misbehave.

Match the route to the season rather than the other way round:

  • October and November: the strongest all-round window. Combine the north, meaning Hanoi, Ninh Binh and an overnight in Ha Long Bay, with a short flight to the centre for Hue and Hoi An. Keep the centre early in the trip if you can, because the rain builds as the weeks pass.
  • March and April: the other excellent all-round window. The north is mild and the centre is at its driest, so a Hanoi plus Hoi An and Hue combination works well. Expect cool evenings in the north and warm beach days in the centre.
  • December to February: head south. Ho Chi Minh City, the Mekong Delta, Phu Quoc and Mui Ne are all in their dry season. The north is still visitable, but expect grey, drizzly Hanoi and cold, foggy mountains, and treat Ha Long Bay as a possible rather than a certainty.
  • May to August: the centre is the safest bet, with Da Nang and Hoi An hot, largely dry and good for the beach, and you can add the south for city and delta time. The north is hot, humid and into typhoon season, so if you go, put the boat trip early and keep a buffer day.
  • September: a transition month that is awkward almost everywhere. The north is still wet and storm-prone, the centre is entering its wet season, and the south is at its rainiest. If you must travel now, favour the north late in the month and keep every outdoor plan flexible.

Whatever the month, give yourself a spare half-day in any coastal or mountain stop. A cancelled cruise, a delayed internal flight or a closed mountain pass is much easier to absorb when the next day is not already booked. If you would rather not rebuild the logistics around the season yourself, tailor-made Vietnam itineraries can be planned around the weeks you actually have.

Peak dates to avoid or book early

Weather is only half of the crowd story. A handful of dates fill domestic transport and coastal resorts every year, and they are worth knowing before you fix your flights.

Tet, the lunar new year

Tet falls in late January or February, and the dates move each year because the festival follows the lunar calendar. Check the exact dates for your year before booking anything. In the week before and the week after Tet, trains, domestic flights and long-distance buses sell out weeks in advance, and many small restaurants, shops and family-run tour offices close for several days. Larger hotels and cruise operators stay open, often with a holiday surcharge. Travelling through Tet can be a highlight if you plan it deliberately, but it is a poor week for improvising.

The 30 April to 1 May holiday

Reunification Day and Labour Day fall back to back, giving Vietnam a long domestic weekend. Beach destinations such as Da Nang, Hoi An, Phu Quoc and Mui Ne fill with Vietnamese families, resorts raise their rates, and flights and trains become scarce and expensive. Book transport and accommodation two to three months ahead, or shift your trip by a week on either side.

Other busy stretches

The summer school break from roughly June to August is peak domestic holiday time on the coast. Christmas and New Year are peak for international visitors in the south and in Hoi An. In quieter weeks, the same hotel or cruise can be booked a few days ahead without stress.

If your dates are locked by school holidays or work, choose the region to suit the dates rather than the other way round. Vibe Vietnam Tour plans routes around fixed travel windows, which is usually easier than trying to force a route into the wrong season.

Crowds and prices by season

Crowds and prices usually track the weather rather than the calendar. When a region is at its most comfortable, it is also at its busiest and most expensive. The shoulder weeks just before or after a peak often give you most of the good weather for noticeably less money and far smaller crowds.

As a rough guide, a mid-range double room that costs around $35–60 (estimate, September 2026) in a quiet week in Hanoi or Hoi An can climb to roughly $70–120 (estimate, September 2026) over Tet or the 30 April to 1 May holiday, and small-group day tours that usually run at about $40–75 (estimate, September 2026) per person can sell out at higher prices in the same period. Treat these as rough planning figures only; actual rates move with demand, and the pattern matters far more than the number.

Where you save is in the low season, and the trade-off is weather risk rather than quality. The central coast from September to November, the north from June to August and the south from May to October all offer lower rates and thinner crowds. If you book those months, favour flexible or refundable hotel rates, keep one indoor option for each outdoor day, and avoid prepaying for anything that a storm could cancel.

Two habits help in every season: book long-distance transport and any Ha Long Bay cruise as early as your dates allow, and leave the last day of a beach or mountain stay unplanned so a delay does not cascade into a missed flight home.

What to pack, season by season

Packing light is easier when you pack for one climate zone at a time. If your route crosses zones, layers beat volume.

Northern winter: December to February

  • A warm mid-layer or light down jacket for Sapa and Ha Giang, plus a windproof outer layer
  • A compact umbrella or rain shell for Hanoi's drizzly spells
  • Closed, waterproof shoes and warm socks for cold, damp days

Northern spring and autumn: March to April, October to November

  • Light layers, since mornings and evenings are cooler than midday
  • A light rain jacket and comfortable walking shoes for city and countryside days
  • Sun hat, sunglasses and insect repellent for Ninh Binh and the mountains

Northern summer: May to August

  • Quick-dry, breathable clothing and a spare shirt for humid days
  • A breathable rain shell and a dry bag for boat trips and day packs
  • Sandals plus one pair of sturdy shoes, and strong sun protection

Central Vietnam: February to April, and September to November

  • For the dry months: swimwear, a light cover-up for temple visits, and a thin jumper for Hue evenings
  • For the wet months: a proper rain jacket, a small umbrella, waterproof sandals and a dry bag
  • A second pair of shoes, because the first pair may stay wet for a day or two

Southern Vietnam: any month

  • Light cotton or linen clothing, a sun hat and high-factor sunscreen for year-round heat
  • A poncho or light rain jacket from May to November, when afternoon downpours are routine
  • Mosquito repellent for the Mekong Delta and long sleeves for evenings near water

Across every region, a refillable water bottle, a small power bank and a universal adapter cover the basics. Modest clothing that covers shoulders and knees is worth having for temples and pagodas, and it doubles as sun protection.

Frequently asked questions

What is the single best month to visit Vietnam?

There is no one month that suits the whole country, but October and November come closest for a multi-region trip. The north is clear and comfortable, the south is drying out and the centre is the only weak point. If your trip is limited to one region, choose March or April for the north and centre, and December to April for the south.

Is it worth visiting Vietnam during the rainy season?

Often yes, if you accept the trade-off. Southern rain usually arrives as a heavy afternoon burst rather than all-day drizzle, so mornings still work well for sightseeing. The northern and central wet seasons are heavier and can disrupt boats, treks and transfers. Rainy months bring lower prices, thinner crowds and greener landscapes, which suits flexible travellers.

How much can typhoons affect my trip?

Typhoon season runs roughly July to November, with the highest risk in the north from July to September and in the centre from September to November. The main practical effects are cancelled Ha Long Bay cruises, delayed flights and trains, rough seas and local flooding. Storms rarely last long, but they do move. Build in a spare day and keep key bookings flexible.

Should I travel during Tet?

Tet is a wonderful time to see Vietnamese family life, but it needs planning. Dates shift each year with the lunar calendar, so check the exact dates for your year first. Domestic transport sells out weeks ahead, some restaurants and small operators close, and holiday surcharges apply. Book everything early and stay in cities or resorts rather than remote areas.

Should I plan a north-to-south trip or stay in one region?

For 7–10 days, one region in depth or two regions linked by a single internal flight is far more enjoyable than three. A north-to-south run works better over two weeks or more. If your dates fall in the wet season somewhere on your route, drop that stop rather than trying to squeeze it in between flights.

How does the weather in Sapa and Ha Giang differ from Hanoi?

Both sit much higher, so they run colder and wetter than the capital in every season. Winter brings fog, wind and occasional frost at altitude, and mountain views can vanish for a full day. Summer is cooler than Hanoi but wetter, with landslide risk on the passes. Spring and autumn are the most reliable windows for trekking and the loop.
